ABSTRACT:

Locating humans and devices indoors is important for many applications, from asset and patient tracking to augmented and robotic navigation.  GPS performs poorly inside.  The dominant indoor localization method is WiFi, where distances are inferred from the attenuation of the Channel State Information (CSI) signals that every access point (AP) emits.  The state of the art is a deep learning model called DLoc that attains sub-meter accuracy by encoding CSI from four APs into a spatial heatmap of the device location. However, to perform so well, DLoc requires 16.5M parameters, far too heavy for edge deployment on the gateways, APs, or phones themselves. 

This paper studies whether DLoc’s remarkable accuracy can be retained at a fraction of its cost. To do so, we compress DLoc into a family of lightweight students: MobileNetV2, a tiny CNN, a variant with U-Net-style skip connections, and a state-space (Mamba) variant. The goal is to learn a smaller model that matches DLoc’s accuracy; the best does better. We evaluate all students for accuracy, robustness, cross-session generalization, statistical stability, and cost. We find that our best model, MobileNetV2-UNet, lowers DLoc’s median error from 0.707 m to 0.640 m, a 9.5% improvement, while using only 2.41 M parameters (6.8× fewer) and 56× fewer floating-point operations. 

MobileNetV2-UNet even matches DLoc on one fold of a leave-one-session-out protocol that tests for generalization to changes in the environment; on others it is always within 0.14 m of DLoc and within the same P90 value. Knowledge distillation improves robustness - models trained with it get 3.4× better median accuracy under Gaussian noise - but only helps if the student has above ~2 M parameters; below that, it harms accuracy. These results show that highly accurate and robust WiFi localization can be done with a lightweight model.

I. INTRODUCTION

Millions of GPS-enabled devices use outdoor localization every day. When people step indoors into malls, hospitals, warehouses, or even their homes, the signal drops and loses accuracy. Being able to perform the same task inside a building or on an individual floor has been studied for two decades.

The strongest current contender is WiFi indoor localization [1], which relies on the Channel State Information (CSI) signals that every access point (AP) emits to estimate distances between AP and device. Because it is based on signal attenuation, it can localize devices to within sub-meter precision.

A state-of-the-art WiFi indoor localization technique is called DLoc [1]. It uses deep learning to convert four sets of CSI signals into an image heatmap that shows where the device is located within the building’s floorplan. To build this mapping function, DLoc needs a large training set and an even larger model with 16.5 M parameters.

The downside is DLoc’s size. Its heavy ResNet model requires high computing costs for training and evaluation because it needs many layers and filters to manage such a task.

Given the success of DLoc as a teacher model that can improve sub-meter accuracy with such high resolution heatmaps, the question that we ask in this paper is if there exist alternative student models that can do similarly well but with far fewer parameters than 16.5 M.

This research makes three contributions:

(1) We present knowledge distillation results for compressing DLoc with a focus on understanding the threshold size below which distilling harms rather than helps and identifying architectural effects;

(2) We introduce several different network architectures for improving computational efficiency and gain insights into what makes a good WiFi localizer;

(3) We demonstrate competitive localization accuracy with respect to both DLoc’s own results and an independently published benchmark on the same WILD dataset [1].

II. RELATED WORK

A. Indoor Localization

Indoor localization is important in areas like asset tracking for safety in hospitals or warehouses or even patient tracking in health care centers.

WiFi signals have long been studied as an indoor solution [1]. The large-scale DLoc results on wireless and localization datasets show that its method works well for CSI-to-heatmap conversion.

B. Knowledge Distillation

A small student network mimics outputs of a large pre-trained teacher network to improve performance in generalization tasks [2].

This idea has received more attention on classification tasks than on spatial problems similar to ours. The teacher outputs soft target heatmaps rather than one-hot maps for location probability distribution estimation.

C. Efficient Network Architectures

Many candidate architectures contain building blocks that have performed well on image-recognition benchmarks.

These include residual connection networks [3], MobileNetV2 backbone networks [4], U-Net architectures [5], and state-space models (Mamba) [6].

For vision problems such as indoor heatmap estimation, researchers studied Mamba [7].

D. Robustness to Distribution Shift

Good performance on clean test data does not guarantee robustness under corrupted inputs or distribution shifts [8].

We create test sets with various types of CSI corruption that are absent from training data to study performance under distribution shift.

III. METHOD

A. The DLoc System and Dataset

On the WILD dataset collected by NIST in Jacobs Hall at UC San Diego (Table I), DLoc takes four CSI images from four APs as input and outputs a single-channel heatmap.

DLoc [1] uses ResNet as its encoder with two decoders: one location decoder for estimating position coordinates within the floorplan and another offset decoder for enforcing consistency across APs, totaling ≈16.5 M parameters.

The predicted location corresponds to the peak value on the sigmoid heatmap output by DLoc.

The WILD dataset consists of CSI images collected from the same building but under different conditions and times over multiple sessions spanning several months (Table I).

We used Sessions 1-2 for training with a total of 17,574 samples.

The smaller Session-2 split with N = 4,260 samples was for in-distribution experiments; the full set was used under a cross-session leave-one-session-out protocol (LOSO) as Session-2 is the only subset from summer.

C. Knowledge-Distillation Framework

Given data pairs with input CSI image I and ground-truth heatmap label y,

DLoc produces heatmap labels y_T without using predicted device locations y_hat; see equation (1).

Both y and y_T are soft label maps showing probability distributions around true positions.

Student models minimize error loss function measuring difference between outputs y_hat and both target maps:

L = α MSE(y_hat,y) + β MSE(y_hat,y_T)

Hyperparameters α and β weigh ground-truth (hard) and teacher (soft) labels.

We empirically set α=0.7 and β=0.3 for all experiments.

Teacher output heatmaps are computed offline; teacher never run during student training.

D. Evaluation Metrics

To compare locality estimation capacity,

compute Euclidean distance e between points at argmax(ŷ) predicted by student and argmax(y) ground-truth:

e = ||argmax(ŷ) - argmax(y)||₂ * 0.1 m (Eqn 2)

CDistances converted via downsampling factor from CSI image pixel units to meters: 0 ≤ e ≤ ∞.

Following [1] use median value of e over test set to minimize effect of outliers;

also calculate P90 error quantile for probability that e < e_{90}

and P99 quantile to filter error spikes inconsistent with human perception;

and finally mean error value e_bar = sum e / N.

Due to quantization effects at scale factor of 0.1 pixel units,

can have different distribution shapes even if same median errors;

hence mean measure needed together with median.
